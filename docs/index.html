Prioritizing Content Pages with Warehouse-Derived Ranking

Evaluating whether historical content, SEO, search-performance, and traffic signals can be used to estimate future organic sessions and improve the ranking of pages for earlier human review.

~79M-row FlyRank warehouse release ~17 months of daily search performance ~70 source clients 323,926 final modeling observations Random Forest regression

Title + Abstract

Question: Can historical content, SEO, search-performance, and traffic signals be used to estimate future organic sessions well enough to improve the ordering of pages for limited human review capacity?

Data: The final study was built from the full pseudonymized FlyRank/internship-warehouse release, whose daily content-performance fact table contains approximately 79 million rows across roughly 17 months and about 70 clients, together with a query-level table. The warehouse was queried selectively rather than loaded into memory as one dataset.

Modeling population: The final controlled extraction began with 418,047 candidate records. After eligibility and quality controls, 323,926 content-page/client observations formed the final modeling population across 53 pseudonymized clients.

Method: Historical signals were transformed into 51 pre-processing features and then expanded to 69 model-input columns. A Random Forest Regressor estimated future organic sessions. A transparent rule-based baseline was evaluated on the same ranking populations.

Result: On the final held-out test set of 18,743 observations, the Random Forest achieved higher NDCG than the baseline at every evaluated depth: 0.9248 vs 0.8113 at NDCG@20, 0.9366 vs 0.8099 at NDCG@50, 0.9369 vs 0.7756 at NDCG@100, 0.8633 vs 0.7244 at NDCG@500, and 0.8197 vs 0.6971 at NDCG@1000.

Use: The evidence supports using the learned ranking as decision support for prioritizing pages. It does not prove that a page will recover after an intervention, that any individual feature causes performance, or that the observed ranking quality will remain unchanged in production.

1. Introduction / Problem

Content and SEO teams may have more pages, opportunities, and potential issues than they can investigate at the same time. The practical problem is therefore one of prioritization: when review capacity is limited, which pages should receive attention first?

This study approaches that problem from the full FlyRank warehouse rather than from the earlier 30,000-row development sample. The warehouse contains historical content, search, and traffic information at a scale large enough to construct a more substantial modeling population while still allowing selective analytical queries.

Decision supported: Which content pages should be placed earlier in a review queue when the team cannot inspect every page immediately?

The final supervised task predicts future organic sessions. Those predictions are then used as a ranking signal. This is deliberately different from claiming that the model directly predicts whether a page is “bad,” whether it will decline, or whether it should automatically be changed.

2. Data

2.1 Full warehouse release

The source is the pseudonymized FlyRank/internship-warehouse release hosted on Hugging Face. The release contains approximately 79 million rows in its daily content-performance fact table, representing roughly 17 months of daily search performance for about 70 clients, plus a query-level table.

The warehouse was not treated as a single in-memory dataframe. DuckDB was used to query the hosted Parquet data and aggregate only the columns and partitions needed for the analysis. This allowed the research workflow to work from the full release without downloading or loading all approximately 79 million rows into RAM.

2.3 Final modeling population

The final extraction identified 418,047 candidate records. After eligibility filtering, 324,998 records remained. After target construction and final quality controls, 323,926 observations formed the final modeling population.

The final modeling population covered 53 pseudonymized clients. A further 1,072 records were excluded after the eligible population was identified because they did not satisfy the final temporal or modeling conditions.

2.4 Temporal scope

The source modeling extraction contained content creation dates from 2024-11-22 through 2026-07-06. After the modeling eligibility constraints, the final content-creation window extended through 2026-04-01. Historical content-update dates extended from 2025-06-01 through 2026-07-06, while keyword creation dates extended through 2026-03-31.

2.5 Signals available to the model

The final feature construction covered keyword and search-demand signals, content characteristics, historical search performance, historical traffic, engagement-derived signals, data-availability indicators, missingness indicators, and temporal attributes.

  • Search demand, keyword length, competition, CPC, and intent.
  • Content type, word count, character count, and category information.
  • Historical impressions, clicks, and average search position.
  • Organic sessions, GA4 sessions, pageviews, and other traffic channels.
  • Historical CTR, organic-session share, AI-session share, direct-session share, and pageviews per session.
  • GSC/GA4 availability and selected missingness indicators.
  • Content age and keyword age, plus calendar-derived components during preprocessing.
Important distinction: The approximately 79 million rows describe the scale of the source warehouse. The supervised-learning population is 323,926 observations after controlled extraction. The paper does not claim that all 79 million daily rows were independently treated as model rows.

3. Methodology

3.1 Research assumptions

The analysis assumes that historical content, search, SEO, traffic, and engagement signals contain information useful for estimating future organic performance. The goal is ranking and decision support, not automated content intervention.

3.2 Target construction

The final supervised-learning target was future_organic_sessions. Historical information available before the target period was used for feature construction, while organic sessions observed during the subsequent target window were retained as the outcome.

Prediction task: historical content + SEO + search performance + traffic signals → future organic sessions

This target definition is materially different from the earlier 30,000-row development workflow, where a decline label was constructed from a supplied trend field. The final warehouse study does not use that decline label as its supervised target.

3.3 Feature construction and preprocessing

The final modeling feature set contained 51 features before preprocessing. These were transformed into 69 model-input columns for the train, validation, and test matrices.

Numerical and categorical variables were processed consistently across splits. Highly skewed traffic variables were represented using logarithmic transformations where appropriate. Missing numerical information was handled inside the preprocessing pipeline, and selected missingness indicators were retained.

3.4 Client-aware evaluation

Client-level separation was treated as a key integrity requirement. The final modeling population was divided into 245,323 training observations, 59,860 validation observations, and 18,743 held-out test observations. Client identifiers were used for grouping, split assignment, and diagnostics rather than as ordinary predictive variables.

The held-out test population was kept separate for final evaluation. The workflow also checked client assignment and preserved the separation between model features and future outcome information.

3.5 Baseline

A transparent rule-based baseline was constructed as a non-machine-learning reference. It assigns a score to pages using available historical performance and SEO signals and produces a ranked list. The baseline is not intended to be theoretically optimal; its role is to answer a practical question: does the learned model improve ranking quality over a manually specified prioritization rule?

3.6 Final model

The final supervised model was a Random Forest Regressor. It estimated future organic sessions from the engineered historical feature representation. The continuous predictions were used as ranking scores, with higher predicted future organic sessions producing higher ranking priority.

The model was not treated as a binary classifier. This matters because the final objective is ordering pages, and ranking quality is more directly relevant than forcing a continuous future-session estimate into a yes/no decline decision.

3.7 Leakage prevention

Future organic sessions were kept outside the predictor matrix. Target fields and other future-outcome information were not used to construct the historical feature representation.

content_hash_id and client_hash_id were retained as keys for identification and client-level evaluation but were excluded from the predictive feature representation. Historical performance variables were used only in historical form.

3.8 Evaluation design

Because the operational use case is ranking, the principal metric was Normalized Discounted Cumulative Gain (NDCG). NDCG rewards placing observations with higher realized relevance near the top of a ranked list.

The final test audit evaluated NDCG@20, NDCG@50, NDCG@100, NDCG@500, and NDCG@1000. Regression diagnostics including MAE and RMSE were also retained to describe point-prediction error.

5. Limitations & Honest Framing

The final warehouse study provides stronger evidence than the earlier small development experiment because it uses a substantially larger source-derived modeling population and a held-out test population. Nevertheless, the conclusions remain bounded by the observed data and evaluation design.

Source scale vs modeling scale

The source daily fact table is approximately 79 million rows, but the final supervised population is 323,926 observations. The paper should not imply that every warehouse row became an independent model observation.

Target limitation

The target is future organic sessions. A ranking score therefore represents predicted future organic performance, not a direct probability that a page is declining or a diagnosis of why a page performs poorly.

Causality

Feature importance and predictive association do not show that changing a page attribute will cause future traffic to increase.

Client heterogeneity

The warehouse is an unbalanced panel. Clients have different amounts and types of historical coverage, and traffic distributions vary substantially across clients.

Zero-heavy outcome

The final population contains many observations with zero future organic sessions. The target distribution was therefore explicitly inspected, and error diagnostics showed that large-volume pages can contribute disproportionately to RMSE.

Generalization

The reported NDCG values are observations from the held-out evaluation population. They should be re-evaluated when new clients, new content types, changed search behavior, or distribution shifts materially alter the data-generating environment.

Human review remains mandatory. The model ranks pages; it does not automatically decide whether a page should be refreshed, rewritten, redirected, removed, or left unchanged.

6. Ranked Recommendations

The practical output is a ranked page list. The model's prediction should be treated as a prioritization signal that helps allocate limited review time.

  1. Start with the highest-ranked pages. Review pages near the top of the learned ranking first because the final test results show stronger concentration of higher-realized future organic-session values near the top than the baseline.
  2. Inspect historical performance context. Review historical organic sessions, GA4 pageviews, GA4 sessions, search performance, and content characteristics before deciding what the page actually needs.
  3. Use freshness and engagement as diagnostic context. Staleness, CTR, search position, and engagement signals can help explain a page's situation, but they are review prompts rather than causal diagnoses.
  4. Separate prioritization from intervention. A high-ranked page deserves earlier attention; it does not automatically deserve a content refresh.
  5. Monitor ranking quality over time. Recalculate NDCG and other diagnostics when new data becomes available or when the client/content population changes.

Recommended operating workflow

Rank → Review → Diagnose → Decide → Act → Monitor

The model determines review priority. A human reviewer determines whether the page should be changed and which intervention, if any, is appropriate.
